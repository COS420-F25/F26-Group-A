# F26-Group-A
COS 420 Group Project

Team Ant

Mission Statement: Team Ants' mission is to build a high-trust, collaborative environment where every team member is empowered to learn, contribute, and communicate openly.

Problem Statement 1:

Students often struggle to learn how to improve their learning and performance while also lacking a supportive community to help them reflect on their experiences. Reflection groups can provide this support, but organizing productive groups can be difficult and time-consuming. 

For example, students may be reluctant to seek out reflection groups on their own. At the same time, instructors and other facilitators may struggle to find and match students into groups that work well together. 

Reflection groups can be more productive when members share similar experiences, challenges, and perspectives because these similarities can make it easier to empathize, exchange relevant advice, and feel comfortable sharing struggles. However, finding these compatible group members can require significant effort from facilitators. 

How might we design supportive tools for matching students into compatible reflection groups so that students can have more beneficial reflection experiences while requiring less facilitation effort?


Problem Statement 2: 

Students who want to improve themselves often try to do it alone, and without outside perspective, it's hard to catch your own blind spots or stay motivated over time. 

For example, I struggled with a concept in a programming class last semester that didn't click despite the professor's explanation. I happened to see a classmate studying for the same exam and worked up the nerve to strike up a casual conversation about their prep; they explained the concept in their own words, and it suddenly made sense in a way the lecture hadn't. The insight wasn't better content, just a different framing from someone working through the same material.

Peer reflection groups attempt to solve this by giving students a space to process and get feedback from others; for example, some courses or clubs run informal peer check-ins or accountability partnerships.

These groups depend on someone taking the first step to organize or approach a peer, and that initiation is its own barrier. Most students won't strike up that conversation on their own, so the exchange that helped me only happened because I happened to be in the room and pushed past my own hesitation.

How can we make it easier for students to find and sustain a small, trustworthy peer group for reflecting on their own growth, without requiring an instructor or organizer to manually coordinate it?
